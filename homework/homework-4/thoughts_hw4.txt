The most clarifying moment in this homework was implementing compute_grpo_advantage by hand. Reading about GRPO, the group-relative normalization sounds like a minor technical detail — just subtract the mean and divide by std within a group. But actually writing it out made clear why it matters: without a critic, the only signal you have about whether a completion was good is how it compares to the other completions you sampled for the same prompt. If all your completions for a given image get reward 0 (all wrong), the advantages are all zero and you learn nothing from that step. That's the zero-std problem, and you can see it in the `frac_reward_zero_std` metric during training. Early on, most steps were zero-std — the model was consistently wrong — and the actual learning only started once the model occasionally got the format right and rewards became non-degenerate.

The format reward surprised me more than the accuracy reward. I expected accuracy to drive everything, but in practice the format reward — just whether the response contained `Answer:` — was what stabilized training first. The model learned to produce the marker reliably before it learned to produce the right answer. This makes sense in hindsight: format is a much easier signal to optimize than semantic accuracy, and once the model consistently produces `Answer: [something]`, you can actually extract a prediction to compare against ground truth. Without the format reward, the accuracy reward would have been noisy even when the model knew the right answer.

Comparing this to HW3's SFT was interesting. SFT loss dropped fast and cleanly. GRPO reward moved slowly and noisily, especially in the first 20-30 steps. I think the difference is that cross-entropy gives you a dense gradient signal at every token, while GRPO's reward is a single scalar per completion that gets broadcast across the whole response. The signal is sparse. That said, the GRPO model's outputs felt qualitatively different — it actually wrote out reasoning steps before the answer, whereas the SFT model just produced the label. Whether that reasoning is real or just stylistic imitation of the training format is harder to say.

One thing I want to think more about for EgoBlind-RA: the reward functions here were simple binary signals. For an assistive agent, the reward would need to be much richer — something like: was the hazard identified, was it communicated in time, did the user change behavior appropriately? Designing that reward function is probably harder than implementing the RL algorithm itself. The algorithm is off-the-shelf; the reward is the hard part.
